## Art kind: sprites and tiles that read at play size

An art board is a sprite sheet for a game: `<meta name="design-board" content="platform=art,width=1280,height=960,tile=16,play=48">`. `tile` is the grid in art pixels (16 × 16 here); `play` is how many screen px a tile is drawn at in the game (48 here: three screen px a pixel). Boards link `tokens.css`, `components.css` and `art.css`; the body starts with `.art-sheet`. A board carries no `<style>`: `art.css` sets the page's margin and ground. The sheet is drawn once, in light; then the check draws every sprite alone at play size on every ground in `.art-grounds`, 1:1, so what a player sees is judged, not the sheet's zoom. App-only notes do not apply. This kind is the art, never the game: no loop, no physics, no level logic. `deploy design export` writes each sprite as its own SVG, one unit a pixel, for the engine.

- **Start from the brief, not the sample.** `init` places `ArtStart`: the kit's sample palette and grounds, and no sets yet. Before you draw, make `art.css`'s `--pal-*` and `--stage-*` the brief's colours and grounds (rename a colour with its `c-*` class and its swatch), then add one `.art-set` for each thing the brief names. `ds/<name>/examples/ArtSprites.html` and `ArtTiles.html` are worked examples of sets, frames and tiles.
- **Pixel art on a whole-pixel grid.** A sprite is `<svg class="art-sprite" viewBox="0 0 16 16" width="128" height="128">`: the viewBox is its pixels, and its width and height are the viewBox times the sheet's zoom (8, `--art-zoom`). Draw it from whole pixels: `<path>`s of `M x y h n v1 h-n z` runs or `<rect>`s, every number whole. No curves, circles, strokes, rounded corners, transforms other than whole-pixel `translate`, filters or opacity: each one blurs or mixes colours in play. A sprite is a whole number of tiles (16 × 16, 32 × 16); a small item still sits in a full tile, with empty pixels round it.
- **One palette, from `art.css`.** Every colour is a `--pal-*` (the art) or a `--stage-*` (the grounds) on `:root`, and every shape is painted with its class (`c-ink`, `c-blue`, …), never a literal colour or `style`. Need a colour? Add it to the palette first, as a variable and its class, and say why on the board. The palette is the one place with many colours: the sheet around it stays neutral UI from the tokens. Keep it small; 12 to 16 colours is a whole game for most styles.
- **Grounds are what the art is played on.** `.art-grounds` holds one `.art-swatch.wide` per background the game puts sprites on, its block an `.art-ground` (`g-day`, `g-dusk`, `g-night`) and its name under it. Show the real ones, dark ones too: a dark outline vanishes on a night ground, and the check draws each sprite on each one.
- **Reads at play size.** At play size a sprite stands apart from every ground it meets: at least half its pixels, or 70% of its outline, at 1.5:1 or more against it, and at least 12 px across. A sprite that melts into a ground gets a light rim or outline there (a second frame, or the palette's light ink), not a hope that the player looks closer.
- **A silhouette of its own.** Everything a player must tell apart (the hero, an enemy, a pickup, a hazard) has its own shape at play size: height, width, a part that sticks out. Colour alone goes at a glance and for players who see colour differently. Frames of one thing share a shape on purpose: keep them in one `.art-set`.
- **Sets and frames.** `.art-set` is one thing in the game, named (`<h2>`) with one line on what it does; `.art-frames` holds its frames in order (Idle, Step), each an `.art-cell` with its caption. Keep a frame's feet on the same row and its body the same size from frame to frame unless the motion changes it, so it does not jitter in play.
- **Tiles repeat without a seam.** A tile is `.art-sprite.tile` (it fills its cell, so it has no checkerboard), its pixels in a `<g id="tile-…">`; beside it an `.art-repeat` shows it 3 by 3 with `<use href="#tile-…">`. Look at the repeat for a line where the edges meet, and for a pattern that reads as a grid (one bright speck repeated every 16 px). Wrap shapes from one edge to the other.
- **Light from one side.** Light comes from the top left on every sprite and tile: highlights up and left, shade down and right, the same on all of them.

- **Sample content and product facts.** A sprite sheet's sample content is the art itself and its names (Hero, Slime, Grass): draw it real and specific, never "Sprite 1". The game's facts (a sprite's speed, damage, how many coins a level holds, what an item does in numbers) are product facts: the brief's, or left off, or flagged with `data-question` on their layer. Worked example: "Slows the hero down." describes the water tile the brief asked for; "Slows the hero by 40%" is a number nobody gave and carries the flag. A reviewer lists each unflagged fact as a `[fact]` finding.

What agents get wrong for game art:

- Drawing with circles, strokes and curves, so the "pixel art" is smooth vector art that blurs in play.
- Judging the sheet at 8× and never at play size, where the detail is gone.
- A dark outline on every sprite and a night level with a dark ground: the hero vanishes.
- An enemy that is the hero recoloured: the same silhouette at a glance.
- A colour picked by eye for one sprite, off the palette, so the game slowly gains a second palette.
- A tile with a seam, or with one bright speck that repeats into a visible grid.
- Writing the game: animation timing, movement or collision. This kind is the art; the engine plays it.
